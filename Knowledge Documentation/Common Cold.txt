## Common Cold
Sources: NHS UK (https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/common-cold/), Health New Zealand (https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/health-topics/conditions-treatments/ear-nose-throat/colds-in-adults), NICE NG237 Recommendations (https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng237/chapter/Recommendations)
Scope note: this knowledge base assumes the GP always sees the patient in person. sources.
 
## Overview
Colds are caused by viruses infecting the upper airways (nose, sinuses, mouth, throat and voice box). They are not caused by bacteria, so antibiotics will not treat a cold. On average, adults suffer from 2 to 4 colds a year. Colds are also common in children, spread easily through coughing and sneezing. A person with a cold can start spreading it from a few days before their symptoms begin until the symptoms have finished. Colds are spread by germs from coughs and sneezes, which can live on hands and surfaces for 24 hours.
 
## Symptoms
- Blocked or runny nose
- Sneezing
- Sore throat / itchy or sore throat
- Hoarse voice
- Cough, often producing mucus (sputum or phlegm), often more noticeable at night and on waking
- Feeling tired and unwell
- High temperature
- Aching muscles
- Loss of taste and smell
- Feeling of pressure in the ears and face
- Watery eyes
- Symptoms tend to peak after 2 to 3 days and typically last from a few days to over a week; an associated cough can persist for 3 to 4 weeks
 
## Assessment (In-Person)
Note: this system assumes the GP always sees the patient in person, so remote/telehealth triage guidance (NG237 section 1.2, "Remote contact with NHS services") is out of scope and excluded here.
 
From NICE NG237, section 1.1 "All first contact with NHS services" (applies to all contacts, remote or in-person):
- In people with a suspected acute respiratory infection (ARI), think "could this be sepsis?" and assess in line with NICE's guideline on suspected sepsis in people aged 16 or over.
 
From NICE NG237, section 1.3 "In-person contact with NHS services at first presentation":
- For people with symptoms and signs of an ARI, use clinical assessment to make a diagnosis and decide whether to prescribe antimicrobials (either immediately or with a back-up prescription — a prescription for use later if symptoms worsen), and offer self-care advice. If pneumonia is suspected, see NICE's guideline on pneumonia: diagnosis and management.
- Consider the person's ARI symptoms and signs in the context of their overall health and social circumstances. The threshold for treatment or referral for further assessment may be lower for people more likely to have a poor outcome, e.g. people with comorbidities or multimorbidity, and people who are frail.
- Do not offer rapid point-of-care microbiological tests or influenza (flu) tests to determine whether to prescribe antimicrobials (testing may still be indicated for surveillance or infection control).
- If, after clinical assessment, it is unclear whether antibiotics are needed for a lower respiratory tract infection, consider a point-of-care C-reactive protein (CRP) test: offer immediate antibiotics if CRP is more than 100 mg/litre; consider a back-up antibiotic prescription if CRP is between 20 and 100 mg/litre; do not routinely offer antibiotics if CRP is less than 20 mg/litre.
- Follow seasonal advice from the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) on managing influenza-like illness.
 
NG237 definition: Acute respiratory infection (ARI) is an acute illness (present for 21 days or less) affecting the respiratory tract with symptoms such as cough, sore throat, fever, sputum production, breathlessness, wheeze, or chest discomfort/pain, with no alternative explanation.

## Prevention
- Wash your hands with warm water and soap, including before eating or preparing food and after touching your face
- Do not share towels, cups, drink bottles, cutlery, or other household items with someone who has a cold
- Avoid touching your eyes or nose in case of contact with the virus
- Cover your mouth and nose with a tissue or your arm (not your hand) when coughing or sneezing, then wash your hands afterwards
- Stay fit and healthy; keeping the home warm, dry, and smokefree, along with adequate sleep and good nutrition, can reduce the number and severity of colds
- The flu vaccine does not prevent colds, and there is no vaccine for the common cold itself because it is caused by many different viruses
 
## Red Flags
- High temperature for more than 3 days, or very high temperature, or feeling hot, cold, or shivery
- Cold symptoms getting worse rather than better
- Shortness of breath or chest pain
- Symptoms not improving after 10 days
- Cough lasting more than 3 weeks, or a wet-sounding cough lasting 4 weeks
- Cold symptoms in a patient with a long-term medical condition (e.g. diabetes, or a heart, lung, or kidney condition)
- Cold symptoms in a patient with a weakened immune system (e.g. due to chemotherapy)
- Rash (spots on the skin)
- Short of breath, noisy breathing, or coughing up a lot of green or blood-stained mucus
- Dry coughing fits that make it hard to breathe
- Unable to keep food or drink down, and not passing much urine
- Pain anywhere that is getting worse despite paracetamol
- Concern about a child's cold symptoms
 
## Management
- Get plenty of rest; feeling pressured to work as normal generally makes symptoms feel worse, and the first 2-3 days are when a cold is most contagious to others
- Drink plenty of fluids to avoid dehydration, especially important with a fever
- Eat healthy food (small children commonly lose their appetite for a few days)
- Paracetamol can relieve fever, aches, sore throat, earache, and headache — follow label instructions and do not exceed the maximum dose
- Decongestants (tablets or nasal sprays) can help with headache and blocked nose, but should not be used for more than 3-4 days (nasal sprays: not more than a week); side effects include irritated nose, dry mouth, and headache; not suitable for children under 6; speak to a pharmacist before use, especially if taking other medication
- Steam inhalation (e.g. sitting in a bathroom with a hot shower running, or a warm bath/shower before bed, or a humidifier) may ease congestion without decongestant side effects — do not let children breathe in steam from a bowl of hot water due to scald risk
- Gargling salt water or a hot lemon and honey drink may soothe a sore throat (salt water gargling not suitable for children); lozenges and sprays may help but are not necessarily more effective than simple pain relief
- Vapour rubs may help ease chesty coughs in babies and small children
- Do not give aspirin to children under 16
- Do not smoke, as it can worsen symptoms
- Avoid combining multiple over-the-counter cough/cold products with paracetamol or ibuprofen tablets separately, as this risks exceeding the recommended dose — a pharmacist can advise on suitable products, particularly for pregnant patients or those with other health conditions
- There is little evidence that supplements such as vitamin C, echinacea, or garlic prevent colds or speed recovery
- Antibiotics are not recommended for colds, as colds are caused by viruses and antibiotics will not relieve symptoms or speed recovery
- Stay at home and avoid contact with others while feverish or unwell; a person remains infectious until symptoms have gone, usually 1-2 weeks (can be longer in babies and young children)
 
## Escalation / Follow-up
Note: reframed from "When to Refer" — since the GP always sees the patient in person in this system, this section is not about deciding whether a consultation is needed, but about what happens next in an already-in-person case: onward referral, escalation, or a safety-net follow-up plan.
 
From NICE NG237, section 1.3:
- The threshold for referral for further assessment may be lower for people more likely to have a poor outcome, e.g. those with comorbidities, multimorbidity, or frailty.
- If pneumonia is suspected, escalation and management should follow NICE's separate guideline on pneumonia: diagnosis and management (not detailed within NG237 itself).
- Follow seasonal UKHSA advice on managing influenza-like illness.
 
From NICE NG237, section 1.1 (self-care advice, applies to anyone whose symptoms can be managed at home):
- Ensure the patient knows the likely duration of illness and when and how to seek medical help — for example if symptoms worsen rapidly or significantly, do not improve over a specified time, or the patient becomes systemically very unwell.
 
Safety-netting (from NHS/Health NZ sources): every patient managed at home should be told clearly when and how to seek further help — for example if symptoms worsen rapidly, fail to improve within the expected timeframe (see Red Flags), or they become systemically unwell.
